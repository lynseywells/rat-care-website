Dealing with Illness

Rats are prone to developing contagious upper respiratory infections (URIs) that can quickly progress into something much more dangerous. There are steps that can be taken to limit the risk of your rats developing a URI, but you still need to be aware of and frequently check for signs of sickness in your rats.

Signs & Causes of Illness

As prey animals, rats are very good at hiding illness so you need to pay extra attention for subtle signs of it. Typical symptoms they tend develop are frequent sneezing, wheezing, crackling sounds in the lungs, and/or red discharge from the eyes or nose. Rat's don't typically have allergies, so these are almost always signs of an illness. In the early stages of a URI you may also notice squinting, puffed up fur, or changes in behavior (notably lethargy and a decreased appetite). I recommend listening to your rats lungs at least a few times a week to catch any illnesses early, because they tend to progress rapidly without treatment.

URIs are usually caused by a flair up of the bacteria mycoplasma pulmonis, which all rats carry in their respiratory system, but can also be caused by external bacteria and viruses. These flair ups are usually caused by stress, underlying health conditions, and irritants in their environment. The biggest contributing factor to developing URIs is a dirty cage; ammonia fumes, poor sanitation, strong smells, and dust can cause respiratory irritation that expose the respiratory tract to bacteria and viruses. In addition, underlying health conditions and stress can weaken the immune system, leaving your rat vulnerable to illness. Rats can also have a URI but not show symptoms at first, meaning any new rats you get can spread disease to the rest of your rats. This is why it's important to quarantine new rats for at least two weeks before introducing them to your other rats.

Illness Prevention

The best way to prevent URIs is to keep your rats cage clean. Clean their cage at least once a week or more (if needed). It's best to wash litter trays, hides, shelves, et c. in the bathtub or outside using an anti-bacterial soap. You can also wipe everything down with a diluted vinegar solution, but this isn't as effective at removing debris and killing bacteria. After washing, I also spray everything in a 1:10 dilution of bleach and let it sit for 15 minutes to ensure all viruses and bacteria are killed (so long as it's thoroughly rinsed the bleach won't hurt your rats). Bleach may seem unsafe, but it was recommended to me by a vet I trust when my rats were sick and hasn't bothered them a bit. Hammocks and fabrics should be washed with an unscented detergent and sanitizing liquid.

Keep the room your rats cage is in dust and strong scent free. Definitely invest in an air purifier. Don't smoke, burn candles, or spray chemicals or air fresheners near your rats. Use unscented bedding with little to no dust. Keep the room quiet without sudden loud noises, to avoid causing stress. Ensuring your rats have a balanced and healthy diet can also help prevent URIs by boosting their immune system.

Most importantly, quarantine new rats for two weeks or more (and wash your hands after handling the new rat). Bringing home rats from of different ages from different places is extremely risky, as I've learned the hard way. A rat can be sick without showing signs for several weeks, and by the time you notice they've likely already transfered it to your other rats. Believe me, quarantining four sick rats is not fun.

What to Do if a Rat is Sick

If you notice your rat showing signs of illness, call a vet immediately. URIs are extremely difficult to fight off without antibiotics, and can quickly worsen if left untreated. Then, quarantine the sick rat at least a few feet away because the bacteria or virus can be transfered through the air through sneezes. If the room has a lot of air movement, move them to another room entirely. URIs are highly contagious, even if caused by the mycoplasma pulmonis bacteria found in all rats. I've read in online forums that it's not necessary to separate rats with a URI because they all have that bacteria and thus can't spread the infection, but this is simply not true. Not only can a mycoplasma infection be transmitted, but you can't know for sure what's causing the infection without running test. The URI could be caused by a combination of bacteria and/or viruses, and it's better safe than sorry. Even though it's hard on you and your rat, you need to quarantine them. Once separated, keep a close eye on your other rats for signs of illness because, by the time you noticed signs, the sickness could have spread.

In the meantime, there is a few things you can do to ease your rats symptoms. A small piece of dark chocolate (at least 70%) can be given every other day to help open up their airways. You can get a humidifier or take your rat into a steamed bathroom to help them breath as well, but ONLY if your sure the URI hasn't progressed into pneumonia (high humidity will make pneumonia worse). I also recommend giving them extra vitamin C to strengthen their immune system. I like to give my rats Natural Science Vitamin C, but fresh foods rich in vitamin C will also help.

If the antibotics don't work (or only help temporarily), especially after multiple rounds, your rat probably has a virus. If it's a virus, your vet may give you an anti-inflammatory medication to ease the symptoms while it runs its course. Viruses are difficult to get rid of, and your going to need to quarantine the ill rat for at least 3 weeks, maybe more. You want the rat to be symptom free for 3 to 5 days before reintroducing them to the group.
